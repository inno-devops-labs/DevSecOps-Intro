# Lab 10 walkthrough — five minutes

I spent a semester treating OWASP Juice Shop as one product and stacking controls around it instead of collecting disconnected demos. We deployed and triaged it, threat-modeled the auth and trust boundaries, enforced signed commits and secret scanning, generated an SBOM and compared SCA engines, ran Semgrep plus authenticated ZAP, scanned vulnerable Terraform/Ansible/Pulumi, hardened the workload under Kubernetes Pod Security restricted with a read-only root filesystem, signed and attested images with Cosign, watched runtime with Falco and blocked bad manifests with Conftest, and finally poured Labs 4–7 into DefectDojo so the backlog had owners and dates.

The point of the arc is the questions, not the logo wall. Week one asked whether we could even operate the target. Threat modeling asked what we were afraid of before tools named CVEs. Secure Git asked whether our change process could be trusted. SBOM and SCA asked what we actually shipped. SAST and DAST asked what the code and the live app admit. IaC asked whether we would burn a cloud account by accident. Containers and Kubernetes asked whether a breakout is contained. Signing asked whether a retagged image would still look approved. Runtime and policy-as-code asked whether we notice misuse and refuse bad YAML before it lands. DefectDojo asked the question managers actually ask: what is due, by whom, by when.

One finding I traced from scanner output to a fix path started as two dashboards that disagreed in format but agreed in meaning. Semgrep flagged Sequelize injection patterns in Juice Shop routes; authenticated ZAP showed SQL injection as a live finding. Lab 5 forced the correlation so we were not arguing about tool preference. In DefectDojo those Semgrep hits appear as separate findings on `routes/login.ts` and `routes/search.ts` plus challenge fixtures — same rule title, different path and line, so they are not “one issue counted six times,” they are a family. The remediation is parameterized queries, a regression test in CI, and until the rebuild ships, the Lab 7 restricted pod, dropped capabilities, and NetworkPolicy as compensating controls. That chain — evidence, reachability, fix, compensation, date — is what I mean by vulnerability management.

Something that did not work cleanly was the ZAP import. DefectDojo 2.58.3’s community ZAP parser rejected our JSON report with HTTP 400 and “please use xml,” even though the lab table lists JSON. I converted the same twelve alerts into traditional OWASP ZAP XML and the import succeeded with twelve active findings. I also had to pin Django/Nginx images to 2.58.3 after `latest` quietly gave me 3.x with renamed behavior, and I published the UI on port 18080 because 8080 was already taken on the host. Those are boring failures, and they are exactly the kind that empty a dashboard if you only screenshot the happy path.

The number I would put on a slide is **569** active findings in one product after importing Labs 4–7, with the Default SLA tightened from 7/30/90/120 to **3/14/45/90** days for critical through low. Median and oldest age are both **0** days because everything was imported today; SLA compliance is **100%** only until the clock runs. Labs 8 and 9 — Cosign attestations and Falco or Conftest denials — do not appear in that 569 because DefectDojo has no parser for them. They still matter, so I would track them as control findings beside the CVE backlog rather than pretend the dashboard is the whole program. If you remember one sentence: we stopped arguing about whose CSV is longer and started arguing about priority, reachability, and dates.
